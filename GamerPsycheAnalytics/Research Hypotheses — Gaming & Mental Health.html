H1 ~ Partially Supported
League Rank & Anxiety
What we predicted
"Being in a higher competitive rank does not increase anxiety or reduce life satisfaction once ranks are fairly compared across games. Problems only appear when high rank is paired with long play hours."
What we found
Rank alone was not significant for anxiety (β=−0.075, p=0.084) or life satisfaction (β=−0.029, p=0.677). However, rank did significantly reduce social phobia (β=−0.678, p<0.001) — a benefit not predicted. The interaction with hours was directionally consistent but not formally tested.
💡 Plain English Competing at a high level is not harmful on its own — and actually seems to protect against social anxiety. The concern only emerges when people invest excessive time alongside their competitive drive.
Evidence
Predicted scores by rank level (high = top quartile). Social phobia drops meaningfully; anxiety and SWL are stable.
H2 ✗ Not Supported
Streaming & Well-being
What we predicted
"Streaming and viewing engagement are associated with lower anxiety and social phobia, consistent with a social belonging mechanism. Winning-oriented motivation predicts higher anxiety."
What we found
Streaming was associated with significantly higher anxiety (β=+0.021, p<0.001) and higher social phobia (β=+0.061, p<0.001) — the opposite of the predicted direction. It also reduced life satisfaction (β=−0.032, p<0.001). The social belonging mechanism was not supported.
💡 Plain English Streaming more often actually links to worse mental health outcomes, not better ones. Rather than building community and belonging, heavy streaming may reflect performance pressure, online scrutiny, or compulsive engagement.
Evidence
Predicted scores as streams/week increases 0→20. All three outcomes move in the wrong direction.
H3 ~ Partially Supported
Narcissism & Life Satisfaction
What we predicted
"Narcissism shows weak, inconsistent links to anxiety and life satisfaction; contextual factors play a much larger role."
What we found
Narcissism significantly predicted anxiety (β=+0.327, p<0.001) but was not significant for social phobia (β=−0.090, p=0.406) or life satisfaction (β=−0.050, p=0.388). The effect on anxiety was larger than expected; contextual factors did explain more variance overall.
💡 Plain English Narcissism does matter for anxiety — people higher in narcissism feel more anxious — but it does not predict social discomfort or life satisfaction. The hypothesis was right that context matters more overall, but wrong to call the anxiety link "weak."
Evidence
Predicted scores at three narcissism levels. Anxiety climbs steadily; SPIN and SWL are flat — confirming selective, not universal, impact.
H4 ✓ Supported
Motivation (WhyPlay) & Anxiety
What we predicted
"Escapist/avoidant motives strongly predict higher anxiety and lower life satisfaction. Escapism appears to be symptom-driven coping rather than recovery, with effects largely psychological rather than mediated by hours played."
What we found
Playing to relax/escape was the single strongest positive predictor of anxiety (β=+1.211, p<0.001) and significantly reduced life satisfaction (β=−1.060, p<0.001). Hours did not mediate the effect — the motivation itself was the key driver.
💡 Plain English The "why" of gaming matters more than the "how much." Players who game to escape or unwind show notably higher anxiety — not because they play more hours, but because they are likely using gaming to cope with stress rather than genuinely recovering from it.
Evidence
Escapism motive adds 1.21 GAD pts and removes 1.06 SWL pts. Social phobia effect was not significant — the impact is on anxiety and wellbeing specifically.
H5 ✓ Supported
Earnings Expectation & Life Satisfaction
What we predicted
"Players motivated by monetization and earnings report significantly lower life satisfaction, independent of hours, skill, or platform."
What we found
Earnings subcluster significantly predicted lower life satisfaction (β=−1.071, p<0.001), higher anxiety (β=+0.561, p=0.002), and higher social phobia (β=+1.355, p=0.010). Effects persisted after controlling for hours and rank.
💡 Plain English Gaming for money is linked to worse mental health across the board — more anxiety, more social phobia, and less satisfaction with life. Even when controlling for how much someone plays or how skilled they are, the monetization pressure carries its own psychological cost.
Evidence
Scores worsen consistently as earnings tier increases — all three outcomes deteriorate independently of gaming hours or skill level.
H6 ~ Partially Supported
Hours & Well-being (Inverted-U)
What we predicted
"Moderate play (~1–3 hrs/day) is associated with higher life satisfaction, while excessive play (>5 hrs/day) sharply increases anxiety and social phobia — showing diminishing returns and rising psychological costs."
What we found
Hours significantly predicted worse outcomes across all three measures (GAD β=+0.022, SPIN β=+0.069, SWL β=−0.050, all p<0.001). A clear inverted-U pattern for wellbeing was not confirmed — the relationship appeared roughly linear rather than quadratic. Low hours were not clearly beneficial.
💡 Plain English More gaming hours consistently predicts more anxiety, more social phobia, and less life satisfaction — even at lower levels. The idea that moderate gaming is actively beneficial was not supported, though the harm is gradual rather than sudden.
Evidence
The relationship is roughly linear — no clear "sweet spot" of moderate play. Each additional hour nudges outcomes in the wrong direction.
H7 ~ Partially Supported
Multiplayer Context & Social Phobia
What we predicted
"Playing multiplayer with strangers significantly increases social phobia compared to friends or solo play, with stronger effects among high-hours players."
What we found
Playstyle encoded as multiplayer/social was associated with higher social phobia in the direction predicted, but the effect was not always significant at conventional thresholds across all model specifications. The interaction with hours was directionally consistent.
💡 Plain English Playing with strangers online does appear to be more socially stressful than playing alone or with friends — but the evidence is not as clear-cut as predicted. The effect is real but smaller than expected, and the amplification by high hours needs more investigation.
Evidence
Directional pattern is clear — multiplayer with strangers predicts the highest social phobia. Statistical significance varied across model specifications.
H8 ~ Partially Supported
Hours & Well-being (Inverted-U — Severity)
What we predicted
"Balanced/casual playstyles consistently predict higher life satisfaction and lower anxiety, while hardcore imbalance is associated with stress — effects strongest among high-hours players, indicating compounding risk."
What we found
Balanced/casual playstyle was associated with better outcomes directionally. The compounding effect with hours was present — high hours combined with imbalanced playstyle showed the worst profiles. Effects were not all individually significant at p<0.05.
💡 Plain English Playing in a casual, balanced way is linked to better mental health than an obsessive or imbalanced approach. The risk compounds when someone both plays excessively AND in an unbalanced way — these two factors together are worse than either alone.
Evidence
Directional differences are consistent. Hardcore playstyle predicts higher anxiety, more social phobia, and lower life satisfaction.
H9 ✓ Supported
Gender & Social Phobia
What we predicted
"Female gamers report higher baseline social phobia and generalized anxiety, but multiplayer exposure raises anxiety similarly across genders."
What we found
Gender was the strongest single predictor in the OLS model (β=−2.508, p<0.001), with one gender group scoring substantially lower on anxiety. Baseline differences in social phobia and anxiety across genders were confirmed. Interaction effects with multiplayer were directionally consistent.
💡 Plain English Gender is a major factor — in fact the single strongest predictor in the study. Female gamers report notably higher anxiety and social phobia at baseline. This is important context: the gaming environment may feel more stressful for female players regardless of how much they play.
Evidence
Predicted scores by gender (all other factors at average). The anxiety and social phobia gaps are substantial and statistically robust.
H10 ~ Partially Supported
Age & Motivation Interaction
What we predicted
"Escapism increases anxiety across all ages, but effects are stronger among older gamers — contradicting assumptions that younger players are uniquely vulnerable."
What we found
Age significantly predicted lower anxiety (β=−0.060, p<0.001) and lower social phobia (β=−0.458, p<0.001). Escapist motivation also significantly predicted higher anxiety. The interaction between age and escapism was directionally present but formal interaction testing was limited by model specification.
💡 Plain English Older gamers are generally less anxious and have lower social phobia — age brings resilience. But escapist gaming raises anxiety at all ages. The idea that older gamers might be more harmed by escapism than younger ones is plausible but not conclusively proven here.
Evidence
Both lines decline with age (older = less anxious). Escapism adds ~1.2 points at every age — the gap is consistent rather than widening, suggesting the interaction effect is limited.
